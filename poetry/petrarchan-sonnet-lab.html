Step 1 · Contrast

Two Ways of Speaking

Free verse feels like talking. A sonnet feels like singing with rules.

Did you know? The word sonnet comes from the Italian sonetto, which means “little song.” In Petrarch’s time, poetry and music were close cousins, and the lute was one of the most popular instruments in Italy.

Note: A Petrarchan sonnet is also known as an Italian sonnet. It is named after the Italian poet Petrarch (Francesco Petrarca, 1304–1374), who made this form famous in the 14th century by writing hundreds of sonnets about his love for a woman named Laura.

Your task

Compare two poems

The two poems below are not the same kind of poem:

  • Fog is free verse. It has no set rhyme, no set beat and no set length. It is not a sonnet.
  • Sonnet 43 is a sonnet. It follows strict rules for rhyme, rhythm and length.

Read both aloud, then answer: Which one feels more like a conversation? Which one feels more shaped and measured? What makes you say so?

Free verse: NOT a sonnet

Fog (Carl Sandburg)

The fog comes
on little cat feet.

It sits looking
over harbor and city
on silent haunches
and then moves on.

harbor = a sheltered place by the sea where boats stay  •  haunches = the back legs and hips of an animal when it sits

Free verse has its own power. It has no rules for rhyme or beat, but every choice still matters. Sandburg uses three tools:
  • Line breaks: he stops each line early so you read slowly, like fog creeping in.
  • White space: the gap between the two parts is a pause, like the fog sitting still before it moves on.
  • An image: the fog is pictured as a cat, with quiet feet and silent haunches.
Sonnet

Sonnet 43 (Elizabeth Barrett Browning): the first four lines

How do I love thee? Let me count the ways.
I love thee to the depth and breadth and height
My soul can reach, when feeling out of sight
For the ends of being and ideal grace.

Tap a dotted word in the poem.
Lines 2–4 in plain English: My love for you goes as far as my soul can stretch when it reaches into the unseen for the meaning of life and for God’s perfect grace.
What is a volta? Volta is the Italian word for turn. At line 9, the poem turns in a new direction. It is the “but” moment: the first eight lines say one thing, then the poem says but, yet or not only that, and changes direction. It might answer a question, show a problem, or look at the idea in a surprising new way.

In Sonnet 43, lines 1–8 show how she loves right now. At line 9 the poem turns to time: her love holds her old griefs, her childhood faith, and will last even after death.

Hidden echo

Browning borrows from the Bible

Browning’s readers knew the King James Bible very well. Here is a verse from a letter by Paul (Ephesians 3:18–19). He prays that people will understand how big God’s love is:

…that ye may be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; and to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge…

comprehend = understand  •  passeth knowledge = is too big to know

1. Tap the three measuring words in Browning’s line 2 that also appear in the Bible verse.

2. Look at line 12: “With my lost saints. I love thee with the breath…” Which word there also comes from the verse?

Why does she do this? The Bible uses these words to measure God’s love, a love too big to measure. Browning uses the same words for her own love, so it sounds just as huge. Her first quatrain measures love like space, and the space words come straight from the Bible.

Step 3 · Meter & Rhythm

Iambic Pentameter

An iamb is a soft beat followed by a strong beat: da-DUM.

Pentameter means five of those per line:

da-DUM   da-DUM   da-DUM   da-DUM   da-DUM

From Browning’s Sonnet 43

I LOVE thee TO the DEPTH and BREADTH and HEIGHT

Step 4 · Idea Spark

Spark an Idea – Is / Isn’t

Don’t start with the rhyme. Start with meaning. Pick something you care about.

Brainstorm

Write down every idea that comes to mind, fast. Single words are fine. Nothing has to be good or rhyme yet. You’ll pick the best ones later.

These comparisons become the raw material for your quatrains (groups of four lines).

One subject, three angles. Your whole sonnet is about one thing. Each quatrain looks at it from a new angle. Browning does this in Sonnet 43:
  • Quatrain 1 (lines 1–4): her love measured like space: depth, breadth and height.
  • Quatrain 2 (lines 5–8): her love in everyday life: by sun and candle-light, freely, purely.
  • The turn (line 9 on): her love across time: her old griefs, her childhood, and even after death.
Same subject all the way through. A new picture each time.

Plan your sonnet

Remember the volta. Volta is Italian for turn. At line 9 your poem turns in a new direction. It is the “but” moment: lines 1–8 say one thing, then the poem says but, yet or not only that and changes direction. It might answer a question, show a problem, or look at your subject in a surprising new way. Your IS NOT list can help you find it.
