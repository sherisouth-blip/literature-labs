Step 1 · Contrast

Two Kinds of Sonnet

Same 14 lines. Same da-DUM beat. A different shape, and a different place for the surprise.

Did you know? Shakespeare’s 154 sonnets were published in 1609. He didn’t invent this form: English poets in the 1500s changed the Italian sonnet to suit English. Shakespeare made it so famous that it now carries his name.

Note: A Shakespearean sonnet is also called an English sonnet. English has fewer rhyming words than Italian, so finding four words that rhyme (A B B A A B B A) is hard. The English sonnet only needs pairs of rhymes, with a new pair in each group of four lines.
You know this one

Petrarchan (Italian)

8 + 6 lines: an octave, then a sestet.

The turn comes at line 9.

Example: Sonnet 43, Elizabeth Barrett Browning

New today

Shakespearean (English)

4 + 4 + 4 + 2 lines: three quatrains, then a couplet.

The big twist comes at the end, in the last two lines.

Example: Sonnet 18, William Shakespeare

Shakespearean

Sonnet 18 (William Shakespeare): the first four lines

Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer’s lease hath all too short a date;

thee = you  •  thou art = you are  •  temperate = mild and calm  •  hath = has

Step 2 · The Form

Shakespearean Sonnet Structure

Three quatrains build an argument. The couplet closes it.

Quatrain 1

Lines 1–4 · sets up the idea

ABAB

Quatrain 2

Lines 5–8 · develops it

CDCD

Quatrain 3

Lines 9–12 · often turns (“But…”)

EFEF

Couplet

Lines 13–14 · the final twist or answer

GG

Tap a dotted word in the poem.
The whole poem in plain English: Should I compare you to a summer’s day? You are lovelier and calmer. Summer has storms, it is sometimes too hot or too cloudy, and it ends too soon. Every beautiful thing fades in the end. But your beauty will not fade, and death will not take you, because you will live in this poem. As long as people are alive to read it, this poem lives, and it keeps you alive too.
Two turns, not one. At line 9 the poem turns with But: summer fades, but you won’t. Then the couplet gives the final answer: how you will last. The poem itself will keep you alive. In a Shakespearean sonnet, the couplet is the last word.

Step 3 · Meter & Rhythm

Iambic Pentameter Again

Good news: the beat is the same as the Petrarchan sonnet. Five da-DUMs in every line.

Step 4 · Idea Spark

Three Pictures and a Twist

Start with meaning, not rhyme. Pick one thing you care about.

One subject, three angles, one ending. Here is how Shakespeare builds Sonnet 18:
  • Quatrain 1: you are lovelier than a summer’s day.
  • Quatrain 2: summer has faults: too hot, too cloudy, and it always fades.
  • Quatrain 3: but your beauty will never fade.
  • Couplet: because this poem will keep you alive.
Each quatrain moves the argument one step. The couplet lands it.
