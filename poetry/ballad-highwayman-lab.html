Step 1 · On the Road

Where Do Travellers Stop?

Before we meet the highwayman, let’s think about long journeys: now, and 300 years ago.

Warm up

Your last long trip

Think of the last long trip you took by car or bus. Where did you stop along the way, and why did you stop there?

Travelling in England in the 1700s

Rough roads

Most roads were dirt tracks full of ruts and holes. Rain turned them to mud, and in bad winter weather some could not be used at all.

Slow going

An early stagecoach went about 8 km an hour: roughly as fast as you can jog. In 1754 a “Flying Coach” took four and a half days to get from Manchester to London. Today that drive takes about four hours.

Stage by stage

Coaches travelled in “stages” of about 16 to 24 km. Horses got tired, so at the end of each stage stood a coaching inn, where tired horses were swapped for fresh ones.

The inn

Travellers ate, drank and slept at the inn. Coaches rolled through an archway into the inn-yard behind, where the stables were. Watch for the inn-yard in the poem!

Step 2 · Highwaymen

Danger on the Road

Between the inns lay long, lonely stretches of road. That’s where highwaymen waited.

“Stand and deliver!”

Stop, and hand over your money and valuables!

Who were they?

Highwaymen were robbers on horseback. They stopped coaches and travellers on quiet roads, often at night, and threatened them with pistols. A horse meant they could strike fast and escape fast.

Famous names

Dick Turpin is the most famous. In real life he was a violent burglar and horse thief, and he was hanged in York in 1739. Claude Duval was famous for his polite manners. The story goes that he once danced with a lady passenger he was robbing.

Heroes or villains?

Stories and songs often turned highwaymen into dashing “gentlemen of the road.” Real highwaymen frightened, hurt and sometimes killed the people they robbed.

Harsh punishment

Highway robbery was punished by hanging. Later, many thieves were sent far away instead, as convicts. From 1788, many of them were sent to Australia.

Why would someone rob travellers?

Some highwaymen were simply greedy or loved the thrill. But many people in the 1700s were very poor, and life could fall apart fast:

No safety net

There was no government help like today. If you were poor, you could ask your local parish for help, paid for by a local tax. The help was small, often not guaranteed, and you might be sent to a hard-working workhouse.

Out of work

When wars ended, thousands of soldiers and sailors came home with no jobs. Men who knew how to ride and shoot, but had no money, sometimes turned to robbery.

An Australian connection. About 160,000 convicts were sent from Britain to Australia between 1788 and 1868. Many of them had been convicted of theft. Australia later had its own highwaymen, the bushrangers, who robbed travellers and coaches in the bush. The most famous, Ned Kelly, was hanged in Melbourne in 1880.

    Step 4 · The Ballad

    What Makes a Ballad?

    A sonnet captures one big feeling in 14 lines. A ballad tells a whole story.

    It tells a story

    Characters, a setting, action and often a sad or dramatic ending.

    It was made to be heard

    Ballads were sung or recited long before most people could read.

    It repeats

    Repeated words and lines, like a chorus, help listeners remember and feel the story.

    It has a strong beat

    You can tap your foot to it, which made it easy to sing.

    A traditional ballad stanza

    This is how an old Scottish ballad, Sir Patrick Spens, begins. Most ballad stanzas look like this: 4 lines, beats of 4-3-4-3, and only lines 2 and 4 rhyme.

    The king sits in Dunfermline town,4 beats–
    Drinking the blood-red wine:3 beatsA
    “O where will I get a good sailor,4 beats–
    To sail this ship of mine?”3 beatsA

    Noyes’s stanza

    Noyes stretches the ballad. His stanzas have 6 lines, long galloping lines, and a rhyme pattern of AABCCB. The short indented line repeats the line before it, like an echo.

    The wind was a torrent of darkness among the gusty trees.A
    The moon was a ghostly galleon tossed upon cloudy seas.A
    The road was a ribbon of moonlight over the purple moor,B
    And the highwayman came riding—C
    Riding—riding—C
    The highwayman came riding, up to the old inn-door.B

    Step 5 · Sound & Image

    Hear It, See It

    Noyes makes you hear the horse and see the night.

    Listen

    Tlot-tlot; tlot-tlot!

    “Tlot-tlot” is onomatopoeia: a word that sounds like what it describes, like buzz or splash. Listen: the hoofbeats get louder, the way the highwayman comes nearer and nearer.

    Activity 9

    Metaphor or simile?

    A simile compares using like or as: “his hair like mouldy hay.” A metaphor says one thing is another: “the road was a ribbon.”
